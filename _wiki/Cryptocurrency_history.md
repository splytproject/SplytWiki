**Cryptocurrency history** combines developments in computer networks, monetary design, online communities and financial markets. Technical progress and rising prices do not always occur together. A blockchain can continue processing transactions while an exchange fails, and a useful software upgrade can arrive during a falling market.

This article follows selected milestones rather than every project or price movement. It distinguishes changes to the networks themselves from changes in the businesses and markets built around them.

## A timeline of selected milestones

| Date or period | Event | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 3 January 2009 | Bitcoin's Genesis block | Established the first block of Bitcoin's main chain |
| 30 July 2015 | Ethereum Frontier launch | Introduced a general-purpose smart-contract platform |
| 20 July 2016 | Ethereum's DAO-related fork | Made governance disagreements over intervention visible |
| 2017 | Initial coin offering boom | Expanded token fundraising and regulatory scrutiny |
| 2018 | Post-boom contraction | Exposed the difference between token demand and viable products |
| 5 August 2021 | Ethereum London upgrade | Changed Ethereum's transaction-fee mechanism |
| May 2022 | TerraUSD and Luna collapse | Demonstrated the fragility of a reflexive peg mechanism |
| 15 September 2022 | Ethereum's Merge | Replaced proof-of-work consensus with proof of stake |
| November 2022 | FTX bankruptcy | Highlighted custodial and intermediary risk |
| 12 April 2023 | Shanghai/Capella upgrades | Enabled staking withdrawals on Ethereum |
| 13 March 2024 | Dencun upgrade | Added a dedicated data mechanism used by rollups |
| 7 May 2025 | Pectra upgrade | Introduced account and validator improvements |
| 3 December 2025 | Fusaka upgrade | Advanced Ethereum data-availability scaling |

Bitcoin's Genesis details are recorded in Bitcoin Core, while Ethereum maintains its network upgrade chronology.[^genesis][^forks] The timeline is selective and is not a claim that no other significant events occurred before the article's review date.

## Bitcoin and the Genesis block

A **genesis block** is a blockchain's first block. Bitcoin's mainnet Genesis block carries a timestamp corresponding to 3 January 2009 and embeds a newspaper headline about bank bailouts. Its nominal reward is 50 bitcoin, but that particular coinbase output cannot be spent.[^genesis]

The historical significance is broader than the reward. Bitcoin showed how a network could maintain a shared transaction history without appointing one central operator to decide all valid payments. Its design combined cryptographic signatures, a chain of blocks and a mechanism for selecting an accepted history. Readers can explore these concepts in [Bitcoin](../Bitcoin/) and [proof of work](../Proof_of_work/).

It is useful to separate *launch* from *adoption*. Creating a network does not instantly create a liquid market, merchant acceptance or a mature ecosystem of software. Those surrounding institutions develop through many independent decisions. Early network history should therefore not be read as an inevitable path to later market valuations.

## Ethereum and programmable assets

Ethereum broadened the idea of a blockchain from a payment ledger to a platform for shared programs. Its 2015 launch made it possible to develop applications whose rules and balances were maintained through smart contracts. Tokens, decentralized exchanges and collectible assets subsequently used that infrastructure.

The 2016 DAO episode exposed a different question: how should a community respond when software behaves according to its code but produces an outcome participants consider unacceptable? Ethereum's fork changed state to address the incident; another chain continued without that intervention. The episode illustrates that decentralized systems still involve people making decisions about which software and history to recognize.

This distinction remains relevant to [community governance](../Decentralized_autonomous_organization/). A protocol's rules can be precise while the social process for changing those rules remains disputed.

## The 2017 ICO boom

An **initial coin offering**, or ICO, raises funds by distributing tokens, commonly in exchange for established cryptocurrencies or conventional money. Some projects sold access to an envisioned service before building a working product. Buyers could therefore be exposed simultaneously to software-development risk, token-market risk and unclear legal rights.

During the 2017 boom, token creation became a widely used fundraising route. The SEC's July 2017 DAO report and its December statement on cryptocurrencies and ICOs show regulators responding to these arrangements. The central lesson was that calling something a token does not resolve whether the offer falls within securities law.[^ico]

The subsequent contraction revealed a practical distinction: money raised is not the same as revenue earned from customers. A project can have an ambitious whitepaper, a liquid token and substantial fundraising while still lacking a sustainable product. A token holder also does not necessarily own a share of the organization that raised the money.

## Upgrades change different parts of a network

A **network upgrade** changes protocol rules. Some upgrades change transaction processing; others change fees, validator responsibilities or data handling. They should not all be described as increases in speed.

Ethereum's **Merge** joined its execution history to proof-of-stake consensus in September 2022. Existing transaction history continued, while validators replaced mining as the consensus mechanism. Ethereum estimates an approximately 99.95% reduction in energy consumption. The Merge itself did not substantially reduce transaction fees.[^merge]

Later improvements addressed different constraints. Staking withdrawals, rollup data and validator operations are separate capabilities. For a beginner, the best way to interpret an upgrade announcement is to ask which constraint it changes and who benefits directly: ordinary users, rollup operators, validators or application developers. [Scaling and Layer 2 networks](../Blockchain_scaling/) explains why a base-layer upgrade can help another network reduce costs without making every base-layer transaction cheap.

## The 2022 crises involved different failures

TerraUSD attempted to maintain its dollar peg through conversion involving Luna. When confidence and demand deteriorated, the mechanism helped create a destructive feedback loop. A peg failure is a failure to maintain a target value; it is not the same thing as every transaction on a chain stopping.

FTX's bankruptcy later in 2022 was an intermediary crisis. Customers depended on a business to safeguard and return assets. That dependence differs from directly controlling a blockchain account. BIS research examining the 2022 shocks found substantial losses among retail participants in its studied data, although that finding does not establish the outcome for every individual.[^bis]

| Failure type | Central question | What continued operation cannot prove |
|---|---|---|
| Stablecoin depeg | Can the token still maintain or redeem at its target value? | That reserves or incentives are sufficient |
| Custodian failure | Can the business return customer assets? | That customer claims are fully backed |
| Contract exploit | Can a flaw redirect or freeze funds? | That valid transactions are economically harmless |
| Market crash | Can holders sell without steep losses? | That past prices will return |

## How market cycles develop

A **market cycle** describes an observed sequence of expansion and contraction. Rising prices can attract attention, encourage borrowing and support new fundraising. A reversal can force leveraged traders to sell, weaken lenders and reduce liquidity. These relationships help explain why losses can spread beyond the first failing project.

Consider a fictional trader who buys an asset partly with borrowed funds. If the asset falls, a lender may require additional collateral or sell the position. That sale can push the price lower, triggering other liquidations. No malicious act is necessary for this feedback to intensify a decline.

Cycles are descriptions, not calendars. [Bitcoin halvings](../Bitcoin_halving/) change new issuance under the protocol, but they do not guarantee a bull market. Interest rates, liquidity, adoption, leverage and regulation can all change between historical periods. A neat chart drawn afterward should not be mistaken for a reliable forecast.

## Reading crypto history critically

Three questions improve historical reading. First, did the event change protocol rules, a company, or a market price? Second, what evidence establishes the event and its date? Third, which explanation is a documented mechanism and which is a later interpretation?

A surviving project can make its early success look inevitable. Failed projects disappear from attention more easily, creating **survivorship bias**. Studying both upgrades and failures gives a more balanced understanding than studying only record prices or popular founders.
