A fiat currency (or fiat money) is a form of money declared legal tender by a government. Its value is not based on physical commodities such as gold or silver. Instead, it derives from the issuing authority's decree and the general acceptance by users. Modern national currencies — including the US dollar, euro, yen, and many others — are fiat currencies. Their purchasing power depends on trust in the issuing state, the effectiveness of monetary and fiscal policy, and market expectations.

## Key characteristics

- Legal tender by decree: A fiat currency is accepted for discharge of debts because a government designates it as such. It is not accepted because it can be redeemed for a commodity.
- No intrinsic commodity backing: Fiat currency is not convertible into a fixed quantity of a commodity. This distinguishes it from commodity money or currencies under a gold standard.
- Supply controlled by authorities: Central banks or other monetary authorities typically control the monetary base and broader money supply through policy tools.
- Value depends on trust and policy: Confidence in institutions, inflation expectations, interest rates, and economic performance underpin demand and value.

## Historical background

Forms of fiat-like money have existed in various guises for centuries (for example, token coinage and paper receipts issued by governments). The modern, purely fiat system largely emerged in the 20th century. Key milestones include:

- The gradual decline of classical bimetallism and commodity-money regimes in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.
- The suspension of gold convertibility for many currencies during wartime and economic crises.
- The final global move away from gold convertibility. The United States ended direct convertibility of the dollar to gold for foreign governments in 1971. This effectively ended the Bretton Woods system and left most major currencies floating.

Since then, floating-rate fiat currencies have been the norm for international finance and domestic economies.

## Issuance and central banking

Central banks manage fiat currencies and are responsible for tasks such as:

- Issuing banknotes and controlling the monetary base (currency in circulation plus reserves).
- Implementing monetary policy to achieve objectives like price stability, low unemployment, and financial stability.
- Acting as lender of last resort and managing financial system liquidity.

Monetary policy tools commonly used to influence the money supply and interest rates include:

- Open market operations (buying and selling government securities).
- Policy interest rates (target rates such as the federal funds rate, discount window rate).
- Reserve requirements and other regulatory liquidity rules.
- Quantitative easing (large-scale purchases of government and other securities) when conventional tools are constrained.

## Monetary policy and price stability

In fiat systems, central banks typically pursue a mix of objectives. Many modern central banks target low and stable inflation (explicitly or implicitly) because runaway inflation undermines the currency's usefulness as a store of value. Tools like interest-rate adjustments and communication about future policy (forward guidance) shape expectations, which are central to stabilising prices.

Inflation arises when the general price level increases. It can be caused by excessive growth in money supply relative to real output, supply shocks, or demand pressures. Because fiat currency supply is administratively adjustable, central banks are the principal actors responsible for managing these risks.

## Advantages of fiat currency

- Flexibility in monetary policy: Governments and central banks can respond to recessions or financial crises by expanding liquidity or lowering interest rates. They can also use unconventional measures such as quantitative easing.
- Easier fiscal-monetary coordination: Fiat systems can accommodate modern fiscal needs (deficit financing) without requiring commodity convertibility.
- Cost and convenience: Issuing paper and digital currency is cheaper than minting commodity-backed coins, and fiat facilitates complex financial systems and credit expansion.

## Criticisms and risks

- Inflation and hyperinflation: Poor policy choices, fiscal dominance, or loss of credibility can lead to high inflation or hyperinflation. Fiscal dominance is where fiscal needs dictate monetary policy. Such inflation erodes purchasing power (examples: Weimar Germany 1920s, Zimbabwe 2000s, Venezuela 2010s).
- Seigniorage and redistribution: Governments earn seigniorage (revenue from issuing currency), which can be a source of public finance. It may also create incentives for excessive issuance and redistributional effects.
- Policy mistakes and time inconsistency: Political pressures or short-term incentives can lead to suboptimal policies that harm long-run price stability.
- Loss of access to sound money: Under fiat regimes, citizens may lose a durable store of value if institutions fail to maintain monetary discipline.

## Fiat vs commodity money and other alternatives

Commodity money (e.g., gold coins) derives value from the commodity itself, while fiat money derives value from legal status and collective acceptance. Proponents of commodity-backed systems argue they constrain inflation and government discretion; critics argue those systems limit flexibility and can cause deflationary pressures in growing economies.

Alternatives to fiat systems discussed in recent decades include:

- Commodity-backed standards (gold or other metals).
- Rules-based monetary systems (e.g., fixed growth rules for money supply).
- Private or decentralized monies such as cryptocurrencies and stablecoins. See [Cryptocurrency](../Cryptocurrency/) for a discussion of these alternatives.
- Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs), which are fiat in nature but use digital ledger technology to provide a central-bank-issued digital form of money.

## International aspects

Fiat currencies interact through foreign-exchange markets, capital flows, and reserve holdings. Some currencies achieve a special international role as reserve currencies (e.g., the US dollar and euro). This role affects global liquidity, borrowing costs, and the issuer country's seigniorage.

Exchange-rate regimes vary between fixed, pegged, and floating systems. A country that pegs its fiat currency to another must manage reserves and often adopt monetary or fiscal constraints to maintain the peg.

## Real-world policy debates

Debates around fiat currency and policy include:

- Inflation targeting vs nominal GDP targeting.
- The appropriate balance between financial stability and price stability objectives.
- The role of central bank independence in insulating monetary policy from political cycles.
- The design and implications of CBDCs, including privacy, financial inclusion, and bank intermediation.

## Case studies and examples

- United States: A predominantly fiat system under the Federal Reserve with an inflation-targeting framework in recent decades.
- European Union: The euro is the single fiat currency for eurozone member states. It is managed by the European Central Bank with price stability as a primary mandate.
- Hyperinflation examples: Weimar Republic (early 1920s), Zimbabwe (2000s), and Venezuela (2010s). Each illustrates how loss of confidence and extreme monetary expansion can devastate fiat currency value.
